Cybersecurity · Lesson 1

The CIA Triad

Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability: the foundation of cybersecurity. Almost every security control, policy and exam question can be traced back to protecting one of these three properties, so this page builds them up carefully with real incidents and practice scenarios.

≈ 35 min read34 quiz questionsLevel: Foundation

By the end you can

  • Define confidentiality, integrity and availability precisely and give controls for each.
  • Identify which property was violated in a real incident or scenario.
  • Explain why hashes, HMACs and digital signatures protect integrity differently, and which algorithms are deprecated.
  • Reason about trade-offs between the three properties.
  • Describe authenticity, non-repudiation and the Parkerian hexad as extensions of CIA.

1The cornerstone of information security

The CIA Triad is a fundamental model that forms the basis for developing security systems and policies. These three principles guide organisations in keeping their sensitive data and systems protected from unauthorised access, tampering and disruption. (In French-language courses you will see the same idea as DIC: Disponibilité, Intégrité, Confidentialité.)

Confidentiality

Protecting information from unauthorised access.

Integrity

Ensuring data accuracy and trustworthiness.

Availability

Guaranteeing access to information when needed.

Key idea

For any security incident, ask three questions. Did the wrong people see it? (C) Was it changed without authorisation? (I) Could the right people use it when they needed to? (A)

The opposite of CIA is sometimes taught as the DAD triad, the attacker's goals: Disclosure (breaks C), Alteration (breaks I), Destruction or denial (breaks A).

The cornerstone of information security

Q1Which word in the DAD triad is the opposite of integrity?

DAD = Disclosure (vs confidentiality), Alteration (vs integrity), Destruction/Denial (vs availability).

Q2Which of these is not one of the three CIA properties?

The three properties are confidentiality, integrity and availability. Authentication is a control (proving who you are) that mainly supports confidentiality. Do not confuse it with authenticity, which appears later as an extension of CIA.

Q3A hacker replaces a university's homepage with a defacement message. The site stays online and no private data is read. Using the three key questions, which property is mainly broken?

The content was changed without authorisation: integrity. It is tempting to say availability, but the site is still reachable; and nothing secret was disclosed, so confidentiality holds.

Q4True or false: in the DAD triad, "Disclosure" is the attacker's goal against confidentiality.

True. DAD is the attacker's mirror of CIA: Disclosure breaks confidentiality, Alteration breaks integrity, and Destruction or denial breaks availability. Disclosure is not about integrity: the data is seen, not changed.

2Confidentiality: protecting sensitive information

Confidentiality involves preventing the disclosure of information to unauthorised individuals, entities or systems. It ensures that sensitive data is accessed only by those who have been authorised to view it. Note that "unauthorised" includes insiders: a nurse browsing a celebrity's medical record without a clinical reason breaks confidentiality just as an outside hacker would.

Why confidentiality matters

In our digital age, confidential information comes in many forms: personal identification data, financial records, trade secrets, intellectual property and government classified information. A breach of confidentiality can lead to identity theft, financial loss, competitive disadvantage, legal penalties (for example under the EU's GDPR) or even threats to national security.

Real-world example: healthcare data protection

In healthcare, patient records contain extremely sensitive information. HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) in the United States mandates strict confidentiality controls. When you visit a doctor, only authorised medical staff with a need to know can access your clinical records: not unrelated administrative staff, not other patients, and certainly not external parties.

A breach occurred in 2015 when the US health insurer Anthem Inc. suffered a cyberattack that exposed the personal information of nearly 80 million people. The attackers accessed names, Social Security numbers, member ID numbers, addresses and other sensitive data, highlighting the serious consequences of confidentiality failures.

Implementing confidentiality

Encryption
Transforming data into unreadable ciphertext that can only be decrypted with the correct key. Use AES-256 (or AES-128) for data at rest and TLS 1.3 for data in transit.
Access Control Lists (ACLs)
Defining which users or system processes are granted access to which objects.
Authentication
Verifying the identity of users before granting access; today this should mean MFA, ideally phishing-resistant passkeys (FIDO2).
Authorization
Determining what an authenticated user is allowed to do, following least privilege (only the access needed for the job) and need to know.
Data classification
Categorising data by sensitivity (for example Public, Internal, Confidential, Restricted) and applying appropriate protection to each level.
Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs)
Legal contracts that prevent parties from sharing confidential information: an administrative, not technical, control.
Three states of data

Protect data at rest (disk and database encryption), in transit (TLS, VPN) and in use (access control, screen locks, and emerging "confidential computing" hardware). Encrypting only one state leaves the others open.

Common attacks on confidentiality

  • Phishing to steal credentials, then reading mailboxes or files.
  • Eavesdropping on unencrypted traffic (HTTP, Telnet, open Wi-Fi without WPA2/WPA3).
  • Misconfiguration: cloud storage buckets or databases left publicly readable.
  • Social engineering and shoulder surfing: reading a password over someone's shoulder.
  • Insider misuse: authorised users accessing data they have no reason to see.
Case study: the Panama Papers

In 2016, an anonymous source leaked 11.5 million documents from the Panamanian law firm Mossack Fonseca to journalists. These documents contained confidential information about offshore entities used by wealthy individuals and public officials, in some cases for tax evasion and money laundering.

Confidentiality failure: the law firm failed to protect its clients' confidential data, resulting in one of the largest data leaks in history. The leak had global repercussions, including political resignations, criminal investigations and increased scrutiny of offshore financial activities.

Lesson: even organisations whose primary business is maintaining confidentiality can fall victim to breaches if they do not implement robust security measures.

Common mistake

"Confidentiality" and "privacy" are related but not identical. Confidentiality is a security property (only authorised parties can read the data). Privacy is about an individual's rights over personal data: whether it should be collected and used at all. A company can keep data perfectly confidential and still violate privacy by collecting it without consent.

Confidentiality

Q1A hospital implements a system where patient records can only be accessed by authorised medical staff using secure login credentials. Which CIA principle does this primarily address?

Restricting who can view records is confidentiality. Access control can incidentally help integrity, but the primary purpose here is preventing unauthorised disclosure.

Q2Which pair of controls protects confidentiality of data both at rest and in transit?

AES-based disk or database encryption covers data at rest; TLS covers data in transit. The other pairs protect availability or integrity.

Q3True or false: a non-disclosure agreement (NDA) is a technical confidentiality control.

False. An NDA is a legal contract, so it is an administrative control. It protects confidentiality, which is why the statement is tempting, but encryption and access control lists are the technical ones.

Q4A nurse with a valid hospital login opens a celebrity's medical record out of curiosity, with no clinical reason. What is the correct assessment?

Confidentiality includes insiders. Having a login does not authorise her to read every record; need to know and least privilege decide that. Nothing was changed (so not integrity) and the record is still usable (so not availability).

Q5A company encrypts its customer database and restricts access perfectly, but it collected the data without the customers' consent. What is the problem?

Confidentiality (only authorised parties can read the data) is fully respected here. Privacy is about whether the data should be collected and used at all, so this is a privacy violation, not a confidentiality breach.

3Integrity: ensuring data accuracy and trustworthiness

Integrity involves maintaining the consistency, accuracy and trustworthiness of data over its entire lifecycle. Data must not be changed in transit, and measures must be in place so that unauthorised persons cannot alter data, and so that any alteration, malicious or accidental, can be detected. Integrity also covers system integrity: the operating system and software should not be secretly modified (for example by a rootkit).

The importance of data integrity

When data integrity is compromised, the consequences can be severe. In financial systems, altered transaction amounts can lead to massive financial losses. In healthcare, a modified dosage or allergy entry can result in incorrect treatment. In legal contexts, tampered evidence can lead to unjust outcomes. Integrity attacks are especially dangerous because the victim keeps using the data and trusting it.

Data integrity throughout the information lifecycle

Creation→Storage→Transmission→Modification→Destruction

At every stage the question is the same: can we prove this is still the data that was legitimately created or approved? Input validation protects creation, access control and hashes protect storage, TLS protects transmission, change control and version history protect modification, and secure deletion makes destruction verifiable.

Implementing integrity

Hash functions
Creating a fixed-size digital fingerprint of data; any change, even one bit, produces a different hash. Use SHA-256 or SHA-3.
Digital signatures
Verifying the authenticity and integrity of digital messages: a hash signed with the sender's private key, checked with their public key.
Checksums
Simple error-detection method (for example CRC32) to catch accidental corruption.
Version control
Tracking changes to documents or code over time (Git), so any change can be reviewed and reverted.
Access controls
Preventing unauthorised modifications through permission systems (read-only vs write rights).
Audit trails
Logging all access and modification attempts for later review.
MechanismDetects accidental changeDetects deliberate tamperingProves who made it
Checksum (CRC32)YesNo: attacker recomputes itNo
Hash (SHA-256) aloneYesOnly if the hash is obtained from a separate trusted sourceNo
HMAC (hash + shared secret key)YesYesOnly "someone holding the key"
Digital signature (RSA, ECDSA, Ed25519)YesYesYes: non-repudiation
Example: verifying a download

A Linux ISO page publishes its SHA-256 value. After downloading, you compute the hash and compare:

terminal

# Linux
sha256sum ubuntu.iso
# macOS
shasum -a 256 ubuntu.iso
# Windows PowerShell
Get-FileHash ubuntu.iso -Algorithm SHA256

If one character of the file changed, the hash is completely different. But if an attacker controls the website, they can replace both the file and the published hash; that is why distributions also sign their hash lists.

Common mistake

Using MD5 or SHA-1 for security. Both have practical collision attacks (a public SHA-1 collision was demonstrated in 2017), so an attacker can craft two different files with the same hash. They remain acceptable only for non-security uses such as detecting accidental corruption.

Case study: the Stuxnet worm

Discovered in 2010, Stuxnet was a sophisticated computer worm that targeted industrial control systems, specifically the Siemens controllers driving centrifuges at Iran's Natanz uranium-enrichment facility. Unlike most malware, which targets confidentiality or availability, Stuxnet was designed to compromise integrity.

Integrity attack: Stuxnet subtly altered the programming of the centrifuge controllers, changing their rotation speeds, while replaying normal readings to the monitoring systems. The centrifuges physically degraded and failed while operators believed they were functioning normally.

Lesson: integrity attacks can be more dangerous than confidentiality breaches because they can cause physical damage while remaining undetected. Critical infrastructure needs special integrity protection, such as signed controller code and independent monitoring.

Integrity

Q1A financial institution uses cryptographic hash functions to ensure that transaction data has not been altered during transmission. Which CIA principle is being protected?

Hashes detect changes, so they protect integrity. In practice the hash must be keyed (HMAC) or signed; otherwise an attacker who changes the data can recompute it. Hashing does not hide data, so it is not confidentiality.

Q2Stuxnet changed centrifuge speeds while showing operators normal readings. Which property did it mainly attack?

It altered controller logic and falsified sensor data. The physical damage that followed reduced availability, but the attack mechanism was integrity.

Q3Which hash algorithm should you use today to protect the integrity of software downloads?

MD5 and SHA-1 have practical collision attacks; CRC32 only detects accidental errors. SHA-256 (or SHA-3) is the current standard.

Q4Which mechanism provides integrity and non-repudiation?

An HMAC proves integrity, but both parties hold the same key, so either could have produced it. Only a signature made with one person's private key ties the message to that person.

Q5A Linux distribution publishes an ISO and its SHA-256 value on the same website. An attacker takes over that website. Why can a matching hash no longer be trusted?

A hash is only as trustworthy as the place you got it from: the attacker simply recomputes it for the modified file. That is why distributions also sign their hash lists. SHA-256 itself has no practical collisions; that weakness belongs to MD5 and SHA-1.

4Availability: ensuring access to information and systems

Availability ensures that information and systems are accessible and usable on demand by authorised users. This principle focuses on maintaining operational performance and quick recovery from disruptions, whether caused by attackers, hardware failure, human error or natural disaster.

The critical nature of availability

In today's always-on digital economy, system downtime can have devastating consequences. E-commerce sites lose sales every minute they are unavailable. Emergency services depend on available communication systems. Manufacturing plants halt production when control systems go offline. The cost of downtime extends beyond immediate financial loss to reputational damage and loss of customer trust.

Measuring availability

Availability is often promised in a SLA as a percentage of uptime, the "nines":

UptimeMaximum downtime per year
99% ("two nines")≈ 3.65 days
99.9%≈ 8.76 hours
99.99%≈ 52.6 minutes
99.999% ("five nines")≈ 5.26 minutes
RTO
Recovery Time Objective: how long the service may be down before it must be restored.
RPO
Recovery Point Objective: how much data (measured in time) you can afford to lose. Nightly backups mean an RPO of up to 24 hours.
Real-world example: cloud service outages

In February 2017, Amazon Web Services (AWS) had a significant S3 storage outage in its US-East-1 region that affected thousands of websites and services, including Trello, Slack and Quora. It was caused by human error: during a routine debugging task, a mistyped command removed far more servers than intended.

This incident highlighted how dependent modern businesses are on cloud services and how a failure in one part of the infrastructure can cascade. Companies with multi-region deployments were less affected than those relying on a single region.

Implementing availability

Redundancy
Deploying duplicate components (disks in RAID, extra servers, two internet links) so operation continues if one fails.
Failover systems
Automatic switching to backup systems when primary systems fail.
Backup solutions
Regular copies of data to enable recovery after loss. Follow 3-2-1 and keep one offline or immutable copy that ransomware cannot reach; test restores.
Load balancing
Distributing workloads across multiple systems to prevent overload.
Uninterruptible Power Supplies (UPS)
Providing emergency power during outages, usually bridging to a generator.
Disaster Recovery Plans
Documented, rehearsed processes for responding to and recovering from disasters.
DDoS protection
Upstream filtering and content delivery networks that absorb attack traffic.
Case study: the 2016 Dyn cyberattack

In October 2016, a massive Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attack targeted Dyn, a major DNS provider. The attack used a large botnet of Internet of Things (IoT) devices, mainly cameras and video recorders with default passwords, infected with the Mirai malware, to overwhelm Dyn's servers with traffic.

Availability impact: many major websites, including Twitter, Netflix, Reddit and GitHub, were unreachable for hours for large numbers of users. The incident showed how vulnerabilities in seemingly unimportant connected devices could be weaponised to disrupt critical internet infrastructure.

Lesson: availability requires protecting not just your own systems but also understanding dependencies on third-party services (here, DNS). Defence in depth and redundant providers are essential.

Ransomware

Today the most common availability attack on organisations: files and servers are encrypted until a ransom is paid. Modern groups also steal data first, so it hits confidentiality too.

CrowdStrike outage (July 2024)

A faulty update to security software crashed about 8.5 million Windows computers, disrupting airlines, banks and hospitals. No attacker: availability failures are not always malicious.

Common mistake

Thinking backups are only an availability control. A backup also restores integrity after tampering, but if it is unencrypted and stolen, it becomes a confidentiality problem. Protect backups like production data.

Availability

Q1An e-commerce website implements load balancers and redundant servers to handle high traffic during holiday sales. Which CIA principle is the primary focus?

Load balancing and redundancy keep the service usable under load or failure: availability.

Q2The 2016 Dyn attack was launched mainly from…

Mirai logged into IoT devices using default passwords and turned them into a botnet that flooded Dyn's DNS servers.

Q3A service promises 99.9% uptime. Roughly how much downtime per year does that allow?

0.1% of 8,760 hours = 8.76 hours. 52 minutes is 99.99%; 5 minutes is 99.999%; 3.65 days is 99%.

Q4Management says: "We can afford to lose at most one hour of data." Which metric have they just defined?

Recovery Point Objective is the acceptable data loss measured in time, so backups must run at least hourly. RTO is the tempting answer, but it measures how long the service may be down, not how much data may be lost.

Q5True or false: availability failures are always caused by attackers.

False. The 2017 AWS S3 outage was a mistyped command and the July 2024 CrowdStrike outage was a faulty software update. Hardware failure, human error and natural disasters threaten availability as much as DDoS and ransomware do.

5The interrelationship of CIA principles

The three principles of the CIA triad are interconnected and sometimes involve trade-offs. Strengthening one aspect might weaken another, requiring careful balance in security planning.

CIA trade-offs in practice

ScenarioConfidentialityIntegrityAvailability
Implementing strong encryptionIncreasesIncreases if authenticated encryption (e.g. AES-GCM) is used; otherwise neutralMay decrease: lost keys make data unrecoverable; small processing overhead
Adding multiple authentication factorsIncreasesIncreases (fewer unauthorised changes)May decrease (user inconvenience, lockouts if a device is lost)
Implementing comprehensive loggingMay decrease (if logs containing sensitive data are exposed)Increases (changes are traceable)May decrease (storage and performance requirements)
Creating system redundanciesMay decrease (more copies and attack surface)Neutral (but copies must stay synchronised)Increases

Effective cybersecurity requires finding the right balance among these three principles based on the specific needs and risk profile of each organisation and system. A hospital emergency system favours availability (a doctor must never be locked out in an emergency); a military intelligence system favours confidentiality; a payment ledger favours integrity.

Exam tip

Some older material (including an earlier version of this table) said that encryption decreases integrity. That is wrong. Encryption on its own does not change integrity; modern authenticated encryption actively protects it. The real cost of encryption is to availability if keys are lost or systems become slower.

The interrelationship of CIA principles

Q1True or false: encrypting data reduces its integrity.

Encryption does not damage integrity, and authenticated encryption (AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305) actively detects tampering. Its main risk is to availability if keys are lost.

Q2Which system should most strongly favour availability, even at some cost in strict access controls?

A doctor must never be locked out in an emergency, so the hospital system favours availability. The intelligence database favours confidentiality and the payment ledger favours integrity.

Q3A company copies its customer database to several extra servers in different regions. What is the most likely effect?

Redundancy raises availability, but every extra copy is more attack surface, so confidentiality can suffer. Integrity is neutral at best: the copies must be kept synchronised, it is not guaranteed.

Q4What is the main way strong encryption can hurt availability?

If the key is lost, the encrypted data cannot be used: an availability cost (plus a small processing overhead). The first option repeats the old myth that encryption damages integrity; it does not.

6Beyond CIA: authenticity, non-repudiation and the Parkerian hexad

CIA is necessary but not complete. Two properties are so often added that many standards (including ISO/IEC 27000 definitions) mention them alongside CIA:

Authenticity
The data or party is genuinely what it claims to be: this email really came from your bank; this website really is your bank.
Non-repudiation
The sender cannot later deny having sent or signed something. Achieved with digital signatures plus trustworthy logs and timestamps.

In 1998 Donn B. Parker proposed the Parkerian hexad, six properties instead of three:

PropertyMeaningExample of a violation
ConfidentialityOnly authorised parties can read itDatabase leaked online
Possession (control)You still control the data, even if nobody has read itA laptop with an encrypted disk is stolen: no disclosure, but loss of possession
IntegrityIt is complete and unchangedGrade altered in a database
AuthenticityThe origin or author is genuineFake invoice email that appears to come from a supplier
AvailabilityUsable when neededDDoS takes the site offline
UtilityThe data is still usefulBackups encrypted with a key that has been lost: the data exists but is useless
Exam tip

Possession and utility are the two properties students forget. Use them for scenarios where data was not read and not changed but the owner still lost something: control (possession) or usefulness (utility).

Beyond CIA

Q1A laptop with a fully encrypted disk is stolen. Nobody can read the data. In the Parkerian hexad, which property was still lost?

Encryption preserved confidentiality, but the owner no longer controls the device and data. That is exactly what Parker's "possession" captures.

Q2An attacker sends an invoice email that looks exactly like it came from a real supplier. Which extension of CIA is violated?

The message is not genuinely from who it claims. Email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) and verification procedures protect authenticity.

Q3Your backups exist, but the only decryption key was lost in the ransomware attack. In the Parkerian hexad, which property is violated?

The data is intact and still in your possession but is useless without the key: a loss of utility. (In plain CIA terms you would call it availability.)

Q4A customer signs a contract with their private key and later claims they never signed it. Which property lets the company prove otherwise?

A digital signature made with the customer's private key, backed by trustworthy logs and timestamps, means they cannot credibly deny signing: non-repudiation. Possession is tempting but describes control over data, not proof of who acted.

Q5True or false: Donn Parker's hexad (1998) adds possession, authenticity and utility to the three CIA properties.

True. The hexad keeps confidentiality, integrity and availability and adds possession (control), authenticity and utility, giving six properties. Non-repudiation is often added to CIA too, but it is not one of Parker's six.

7Practice: which property was violated?

Decide before opening each answer.

An attacker intercepts a payment request and changes the IBAN to his own account.

Integrity (and authenticity of the request). Defence: signed or authenticated messages, plus out-of-band confirmation of changed bank details.

An employee emails a customer list to a personal Gmail account before leaving the company.

Confidentiality. Data reached an unauthorised place. Controls: data-loss prevention, least privilege, NDA and exit procedures.

A student's script floods the university registration portal on enrolment day, so nobody can register.

Availability. Controls: rate limiting, load balancing, DDoS protection.

A cloud administrator accidentally makes a storage bucket of passport scans public.

Confidentiality through misconfiguration (OWASP: Security Misconfiguration). No attacker is needed for a breach.

A company receives an email "from the CEO" asking finance to urgently pay a new supplier.

Authenticity (business email compromise). If the payment is made, integrity of the payment process is also broken. Defence: verify by phone using a known number, DMARC/SPF/DKIM on email.

Ransomware encrypts a hospital's servers and the group posts patient files on its leak site.

Availability and confidentiality: the double-extortion pattern. Offline backups restore availability but cannot undo the disclosure.

8Final review

Final review: The CIA triad

Q1Ransomware has encrypted your file servers. Which single control best restores both availability and integrity of the data?

Clean backups that ransomware cannot reach bring the data back (availability) in its original state (integrity). TLS protects data in transit and a load balancer spreads traffic; neither recovers encrypted files.

Q2A former employee's account was never disabled, and it is used to download the customer list. Which property is violated, and which control would have prevented it?

Data reached someone no longer authorised to see it: confidentiality. Exit procedures and least privilege close that door. Hashes detect changes (integrity), not disclosure.

Q3True or false: a single control can affect more than one CIA property, sometimes in opposite directions.

True. MFA increases confidentiality and integrity but may decrease availability (lockouts, a lost phone). That is why the trade-off table matters: you balance the three, you do not maximise one in isolation.

Q4An attacker silently changes a grade in the university database and nobody notices for months. Which pair of controls would best detect the change?

Integrity attacks are dangerous because the data keeps being trusted; logs and integrity checks make the change visible. Encryption and NDAs protect confidentiality, while UPS, RAID and load balancing protect availability.

Q5Which of these incidents was caused by human error rather than an attacker?

A mistyped command during routine debugging removed far more S3 servers than intended. Dyn (Mirai botnet), Stuxnet (a state-level worm) and Anthem (a cyberattack) all had attackers behind them.

Q6A phishing email steals an employee's password and the attacker reads her mailbox. Which property is broken, and which control best prevents a repeat?

The wrong person read private data: confidentiality. Passkeys are bound to the real website, so a stolen password alone no longer gets in. Checksums only catch accidental corruption and do nothing against a valid login.

Summary

  • Confidentiality: only authorised people see data. Controls: encryption (AES-256, TLS 1.3), MFA/passkeys, least privilege, classification, NDAs.
  • Integrity: data and systems are accurate and unaltered, and changes are detectable. Controls: SHA-256 hashes, HMACs, digital signatures, access control, version control, audit trails. Avoid MD5 and SHA-1.
  • Availability: usable when needed. Controls: redundancy, failover, load balancing, tested offline/immutable backups, UPS, DR plans, DDoS protection. Measure with uptime, RTO and RPO.
  • The three trade off against each other; the right balance depends on the system (hospital vs bank vs intelligence agency).
  • DAD (Disclosure, Alteration, Destruction) is the attacker's view of CIA.
  • Extensions: authenticity and non-repudiation; Parker's hexad adds possession, authenticity and utility.
