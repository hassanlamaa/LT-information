Cybersecurity Fundamentals

Master the principles and practices of information security with real-world examples. This chapter has three lessons: the foundations (what we protect and why), the technical measures that do the protecting, and the risk-management thinking that decides where to spend effort.

Lesson 1 · Foundations

1Cyberspace and security

Cyberspace extends our natural environment, reflecting society through digital infrastructures, data and networked services. Unlike physical spaces, it is a pure creation of humans that does not belong to nature. Everything in it (servers, cables, protocols, accounts, databases) was designed by people, which means it also contains the mistakes people make.

Cybersecurity is the set of measures (technical, organisational, human and legal) that protect the things of value in cyberspace. Those things of value are called assets: customer data, a payment service, a company's reputation, even the availability of a hospital's scheduling system.

Real-world example

Social media platforms: Facebook, X (formerly Twitter) and LinkedIn create digital social environments that mirror our physical social interactions, but with different rules and risks: fake profiles, account takeover, mass data scraping, misinformation.

E-commerce: Amazon and eBay have created digital marketplaces that function like physical shopping malls but with global reach and different security challenges: card fraud, fake sellers, credential-stuffing attacks on customer accounts.

Analogy

A physical shop worries about burglars at night. An online shop has "burglars" trying the door every second, from every country at once, and they can try thousands of stolen passwords per minute. Same goals (protect stock and customers), very different scale.

Common mistake

Treating cybersecurity as "an IT problem". Cyberspace reflects society, so most incidents combine a technical flaw with a human or organisational one: a phished employee, a skipped patch, an unmanaged supplier account.

2The DIC criteria (the CIA triad)

The DIC criteria form the foundation of cybersecurity. "DIC" comes from the French Disponibilité, Intégrité, Confidentialité; in English textbooks the same three ideas are called the CIA triad. Same concepts, different order.

Availability (D)
Resources must be operational and accessible to authorised users when needed.
Integrity (I)
Resources remain intact, accurate and unmodified without authorisation, and any change is detectable.
Confidentiality (C)
Data is protected against unauthorised disclosure: only those allowed to read it can read it.
CriterionQuestion it answersTypical attackTypical control
AvailabilityCan I use it when I need it?DDoS, ransomware, sabotageRedundancy, backups, DDoS protection
IntegrityCan I trust it has not been changed?Tampering, man-in-the-middle, wiper malwareHashes, digital signatures, access control
ConfidentialityIs it seen only by the right people?Data breach, eavesdropping, phishingEncryption, MFA, least privilege

Practical examples

Availability failure

In October 2016 a DDoS attack on Dyn, a DNS provider, used the Mirai botnet of hacked cameras and video recorders. Twitter, Netflix, Reddit and GitHub were unreachable for hours for many users. Nothing was stolen; the service simply could not be used.

Integrity (and more) breach

The 2014 Sony Pictures attack used wiper malware that destroyed data on thousands of machines, and the attackers leaked emails and unreleased films. That is an integrity and availability failure (destroyed data) and a confidentiality failure (leaks).

Confidentiality compromise

The 2017 Equifax breach exposed personal data (names, Social Security numbers, birth dates) of about 147 million people: a massive confidentiality failure.

Exam tip

When asked "which criterion was violated?", ask what the victim lost: the ability to use it (availability), the ability to trust it (integrity), or control over who sees it (confidentiality). Many real incidents hit more than one; name the main one and justify it.

Scenario: a student changes his grade in the university database

Integrity. The data is still available and was not necessarily disclosed, but it is no longer accurate. If he also downloaded other students' grades, confidentiality was violated too.

Scenario: ransomware encrypts a clinic's files and the attackers publish patient records

Availability (files unusable) and confidentiality (records leaked). This "double extortion" pattern, encrypting and stealing, is now the normal ransomware model.

3Additional security functions

Modern cybersecurity extends beyond the DIC criteria to include functions that tie actions to people:

Identification
Claiming an identity: typing a username, presenting a badge.
Authentication
Proving that claim: password, fingerprint, security key, passkey.
Authorization
Deciding what the authenticated identity may do (read, write, approve).
Non-repudiation
The author of an action cannot credibly deny it later, usually because it carries their digital signature.
Accountability
Every action can be traced to one responsible person; impossible with shared accounts.
Traceability and auditing
Recording activities in logs (audit trails) so they can be reviewed and analysed later.

Authentication factors and MFA

An authentication factor is a category of proof: something you know (password, PIN), something you have (phone, hardware key), something you are (fingerprint, face). MFA requires two or more different categories. Two passwords are still one factor.

MethodStrengthWhy
Password onlyWeakPhished, guessed, reused across sites (credential stuffing)
SMS one-time codeBetter than nothingSIM-swap and real-time phishing can steal the code
Authenticator app / pushGoodPush can be abused by "MFA fatigue" (spamming approvals); number matching helps
Passkey / FIDO2 security keyStrongest common optionPhishing-resistant: the key is cryptographically bound to the real website, so a fake site gets nothing usable
Case study: online banking security

Modern banking systems implement all these security functions:

  • Authentication: multi-factor authentication using phones (app approval or one-time codes), increasingly passkeys.
  • Non-repudiation: digital signatures on transactions.
  • Traceability: complete audit trails of all transactions.

When a customer disputes a transaction, the bank can provide strong evidence through these security measures: who logged in, from which device, which transaction was signed, and when.

Common mistake

Confusing authentication with authorization. Logging in successfully (authentication) says nothing about what you are allowed to do. A logged-in student who can open the admin page is an authorization failure, the most common web flaw (OWASP "Broken Access Control").

Key takeaway

Cybersecurity begins with understanding the fundamental principles of availability, integrity and confidentiality, and extends to physical protection of infrastructure. These principles apply to all digital systems regardless of their specific purpose or technology.

Lesson 2 · Technical security

4Logical security

Logical security is the implementation of security mechanisms through software to ensure proper functioning of programs, services and data protection:

  • Quality software development and security testing
  • Cryptography for integrity and confidentiality
  • Access control and authentication procedures
  • Malware and intrusion detection systems (antivirus/EDR, IDS/IPS)
Real-world example: HTTPS encryption

When you visit a secure website (HTTPS, padlock icon), logical security measures are at work:

  • Encryption: the TLS protocol (today TLS 1.3, or 1.2) encrypts data between your browser and the server. "SSL" is the old name; all SSL versions and TLS 1.0/1.1 are deprecated and insecure.
  • Authentication: digital certificates issued by a certificate authority verify the website's identity.
  • Integrity: authenticated encryption (for example AES-GCM) and keyed hashes ensure data is not modified in transit.

Current versus deprecated cryptography

PurposeUse todayDeprecated / broken
Symmetric encryptionAES-128/256 (AES-GCM), ChaCha20-Poly1305DES (56-bit key, brute-forced in the late 1990s), 3DES, RC4
HashingSHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-3MD5 and SHA-1 (practical collisions exist)
Password storageArgon2id, bcrypt, scrypt (slow, salted)Plain MD5/SHA-1/SHA-256 of the password, or plaintext
Secure web trafficTLS 1.3, TLS 1.2SSL 2.0/3.0, TLS 1.0/1.1
Public-key / signaturesRSA ≥ 2048-bit, ECDSA/Ed25519; NIST post-quantum standards (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, 2024) being rolled outRSA-1024 and smaller
Common mistake

"Hashing encrypts the password." No: encryption is reversible with a key; a hash is one-way. And a plain fast hash (even SHA-256) is the wrong tool for passwords, because attackers can try billions of guesses per second. Use a deliberately slow password-hashing function such as Argon2id or bcrypt.

5Application security

Security by design means security is integrated throughout the software development lifecycle (a "secure SDLC"):

  • Development methodology following standards (threat modelling, secure coding guidelines)
  • Application robustness and quality controls (code review, static and dynamic testing)
  • Security mechanism integration (authentication, input validation, logging)
  • Regular security updates and patches, including for third-party libraries

OWASP Top 10

The OWASP Top 10 is the reference list of the most critical web application risks. The 2021 edition, still the one most courses and exams cite:

A04Insecure DesignPassword reset that only asks "what is your favourite colour?"

Update

OWASP published a 2025 edition. Broken Access Control stays at number 1, and new categories reflect current attacks, notably Software Supply Chain Failures and Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions. Check which edition your exam uses; the 2021 list above is the safe default.

Case study: the Equifax breach (2017)

Equifax suffered a massive data breach exposing personal information of about 147 million people. The cause was an unpatched vulnerability in the Apache Struts web framework (CVE-2017-5638). A fix had been published in March 2017; attackers got in around mid-May.

Security failures:

  • Failure to apply a known security patch for about two months.
  • Inadequate vulnerability scanning and detection (an expired certificate on a traffic-inspection device meant the data theft went unnoticed for weeks).
  • Poor segmentation of sensitive data, so one entry point reached many databases.

This case highlights the critical importance of application security practices, especially timely patching. The same lesson repeated with Log4Shell (December 2021, a flaw in the widely used Log4j Java logging library) and the MOVEit Transfer mass data theft (2023, a SQL injection flaw exploited against many organisations at once).

6Operational security

Operational security ensures proper functioning of computer systems and telecommunications networks through:

  • Computer park (asset inventory) management: you cannot protect devices you do not know you have
  • Configuration and update management
  • Incident management and resolution
  • Backup, continuity and maintenance

Incident response

An incident is any event that harms or threatens CIA. The classic lifecycle (NIST SP 800-61) is: Preparation → Detection & Analysis → Containment, Eradication & Recovery → Post-incident activity (lessons learned). The 2025 revision of SP 800-61 re-maps these activities onto the NIST CSF 2.0 functions, but the phases below are still how practitioners talk.

  1. Detection: notice and confirm something is wrong (alerts, logs, user report).
  2. Containment: stop it spreading: isolate machines, disable accounts.
  3. Eradication: remove the cause: malware, backdoors, the exploited vulnerability.
  4. Recovery: restore from clean backups, monitor closely, return to normal.

Common security incident response timeline (illustrative targets; real durations vary enormously with the incident and the organisation's preparation):

PhaseTypical target durationMain goal
Detection0–2 hoursConfirm the incident and its scope
Containment2–24 hoursStop the spread
Eradication1–7 daysRemove the attacker and the cause
Recovery1–4 weeksReturn to normal, safely

Backups that survive ransomware

Modern ransomware groups look for backups and delete or encrypt them first. The 3-2-1 rule: keep 3 copies of data, on 2 different media, with 1 copy off-site. Current practice adds at least one offline or immutable copy (cannot be altered even by an admin account) and regular restore tests.

Real-world example: ransomware attack response

When the City of Atlanta was hit by SamSam ransomware in March 2018, its operational security practices were tested:

  • Backup systems: some departments could restore from backups.
  • Gaps identified: inconsistent backup practices across departments.
  • Recovery time: some systems took weeks to restore.
  • Cost: early emergency recovery contracts alone were reported at about $2.7 million; later estimates of the total cost were considerably higher. The ransom demand itself was far smaller than the recovery bill.
Ransomware and phishing trends

Today's ransomware is usually run as "ransomware-as-a-service" (developers rent the malware to affiliates) and uses double extortion: steal data, encrypt systems, then threaten to publish. Some groups skip encryption and only steal. The usual entry points are phishing, stolen credentials on remote-access services without MFA (as in the 2021 Colonial Pipeline attack), and unpatched internet-facing systems. Phishing itself has moved to AI-written, well-targeted emails, QR-code phishing, and "adversary-in-the-middle" kits that steal session cookies, which is why phishing-resistant MFA (passkeys/FIDO2) is now recommended.

Key insight

Global security is always that of the weakest link. Implementing encryption for data transfer is ineffective if the data can be accessed through unsecured platforms. A comprehensive approach must address all layers of the technology stack.

Lesson 3 · Risk management

7Threats, vulnerabilities and risk

Three words students mix up constantly. Get these right and most risk questions become easy.

TermDefinitionExample (laptop)
ThreatSomething that could cause harm: an actor or eventA thief in a café
VulnerabilityA weakness the threat can exploitThe disk is not encrypted
RiskThe chance that the threat exploits the vulnerability, combined with the damage if it doesCustomer files exposed if the laptop is stolen
ControlA measure that reduces riskFull-disk encryption (BitLocker/FileVault)
Key idea

Risk = Likelihood × Impact. No threat, or no vulnerability, means (almost) no risk. You usually cannot remove threats, so you reduce vulnerabilities or reduce impact.

Worked example

Score likelihood and impact from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high):

RiskLikelihoodImpactScore
Phishing leads to email account takeover4416
Stolen unencrypted laptop with client data3412
Flood destroys the server room155

Treat phishing first. If you add full-disk encryption, a stolen laptop is still likely (3) but the impact drops to 2: the new score 3 × 2 = 6 is the residual risk. The flood is rare but catastrophic: a good candidate for transfer (insurance) plus off-site backups.

Cyber threat levels

Cybersecurity threats can be categorised by their potential impact:

  • Low danger: nuisances like spam, minor disruptions.
  • Medium danger: controllable impacts requiring significant resources.
  • High danger: severe damages with lasting consequences.

Threat probability vs impact matrix:

Impact ↓ / Probability →LowMediumHigh
HighMedium riskHigh riskCritical risk
MediumLow riskMedium riskHigh risk
LowLow riskLow riskMedium risk

Risk treatment options

Mitigate (reduce)

Add controls: MFA, patching, encryption.

Transfer (share)

Cyber insurance, outsourcing to a provider under contract.

Avoid

Stop the risky activity: do not store card numbers at all.

Accept

Consciously live with a low risk, documented and signed off by management.

Case study: NotPetya (2017)

NotPetya caused an estimated $10 billion or more in damages worldwide, hitting companies such as Maersk, Merck and FedEx's TNT Express. It looked like ransomware, but it was really a wiper: paying the ransom could not restore the files.

Threat characteristics:

  • High impact: paralysed operations for major corporations (Maersk had to rebuild much of its IT estate).
  • Global reach: affected organisations in many countries, though it started in Ukraine.
  • Rapid spread: used multiple infection vectors, including a compromised update of the Ukrainian accounting software M.E.Doc, the EternalBlue exploit and stolen credentials.

This attack demonstrated how a high-danger threat could disrupt global supply chains and cause massive financial losses.

Common mistake

Calling an unpatched server "a threat". It is a vulnerability. The threat is whoever (or whatever) might exploit it.

8Types of security controls

Controls are classified two ways at once: by when they act (before, during, after an incident) and by what kind of measure they are.

Preventive (stop it)Detective (notice it)Corrective (fix it)
AdministrativeSecurity policy, awareness training, background checksAudits, log reviews, job rotationIncident response plan, disciplinary process
TechnicalFirewall, MFA, encryption, patchingIDS, SIEM alerts, antivirus/EDR detectionRestore from backup, EDR quarantine, patch after incident
PhysicalLocks, badge readers, fencesCCTV, motion sensors, alarmsFire suppression, replacing damaged hardware

Two more types appear in exams: deterrent controls discourage attackers (warning banners, visible cameras) and compensating controls substitute when the ideal one is impossible (extra monitoring on a legacy system that cannot be patched).

Exam tip

Classify on both axes: "CCTV camera" = physical, detective (and deterrent if visible). "Security awareness training" = administrative, preventive. "Restoring from backup" = technical, corrective.

9Global cyber risks

Modern organisations face cybersecurity challenges that extend beyond technical vulnerabilities:

  • Dependence on foreign technology suppliers (cloud, chips, software)
  • Economic intelligence and corporate espionage
  • Environmental impacts of digital infrastructure (energy, e-waste)
  • Regulatory compliance across jurisdictions (for example GDPR in the EU, NIS2 for critical sectors in the EU)
Real-world example: SolarWinds supply chain attack

In 2020, attackers compromised SolarWinds' software build system, inserting a backdoor (SUNBURST) into Orion updates. About 18,000 customers installed the trojanised update, including government agencies; a much smaller set were then actively exploited.

Global risk factors:

  • Supply chain vulnerability: attack through a trusted software supplier.
  • Attribution challenges: sophisticated state-sponsored actors.
  • Trust erosion: compromised trust in software update mechanisms.

This incident highlighted how global dependencies create systemic risks that transcend individual organisational security.

XZ Utils backdoor (2024)

A backdoor was slipped into a widely used open-source compression library by a contributor who had gained maintainers' trust over a long period. It was caught by chance before reaching most stable Linux releases. Supply-chain risk includes open source.

CrowdStrike outage (2024)

A faulty security-software update crashed about 8.5 million Windows machines worldwide, grounding flights and disrupting hospitals. No attacker involved: dependence on one supplier is itself an availability risk.

10Security architecture dimensions and frameworks

Effective cybersecurity requires addressing multiple dimensions simultaneously:

  • Political and managerial: security policy, governance, risk management.
  • Technical: security tools, systems, infrastructure.
  • Human: training, awareness, ethics, competence.
  • Legal and regulatory: compliance, standards, procedures.
Case study: Target data breach (2013)

Target suffered a massive data breach exposing about 40 million payment cards and personal records of up to 70 million customers.

Multi-dimensional failure analysis:

  • Technical: attackers entered with stolen credentials of an HVAC (heating and air-conditioning) vendor, then moved to the payment network.
  • Managerial: alerts from the security monitoring tools were not acted on.
  • Human: vendor access management was inadequate.
  • Legal: resulted in an $18.5 million multi-state settlement (2017), on top of other settlements and costs.

This case demonstrates how failures across multiple dimensions can compound to create a major security incident.

Frameworks that organise the work

NIST CSF 2.0 (2024)

Six functions: Govern (new in 2.0: strategy, roles, policy, supply-chain risk), Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Version 2.0 also widened the scope from critical infrastructure to every organisation.

ISO/IEC 27001:2022

International, certifiable standard for an Information Security Management System (ISMS). Annex A has 93 controls in four themes: organisational, people, physical, technological. Certificates against the 2013 version had to transition by October 2025.

Zero trust

"Never trust, always verify": no user or device is trusted just because it is inside the network. Every request is authenticated, authorised and checked for device health (NIST SP 800-207).

Govern→Identify→Protect→Detect→Respond→Recover
Exam tip

Older material lists five CSF functions. CSF 2.0 (February 2024) has six; the new one is Govern, which sits at the centre of the other five.

Course conclusion

Cybersecurity requires a global, multidisciplinary approach that integrates technical measures with organisational, human, legal and ethical dimensions. No single solution can provide complete protection; effective security emerges from the thoughtful integration of people, processes and technology across all dimensions of the organisation.

11Check your understanding

Quiz: Cybersecurity fundamentals

Q1The 2016 Mirai attack on Dyn made major websites unreachable for hours. Which criterion was mainly violated?

Nothing was read or changed; users simply could not reach the services. Denial of service is the textbook availability attack.

Q2In the French acronym DIC, what does the "D" stand for?

DIC = Disponibilité, Intégrité, Confidentialité: the same three criteria as the English CIA triad.

Q3A user types her username on a login page. Which function is that?

A username is a claim of identity. Authentication happens when she proves it (password, passkey). Authorization decides what she may do afterwards.

Q4Which mechanism best provides non-repudiation for a bank transfer?

Only the holder of the private key can produce the signature, so they cannot credibly deny it. A plain hash proves nothing about who made it, encryption gives confidentiality, and a shared password destroys accountability.

Q5Which authentication method resists phishing best?

A passkey is cryptographically bound to the genuine website's domain, so a look-alike phishing site cannot obtain a usable login. SMS codes and push approvals can be relayed or tricked out of users.

Q6What was the root cause of the 2017 Equifax breach?

A patch for the Struts flaw existed about two months before attackers used it. This is OWASP A06, Vulnerable and Outdated Components.

Q7You must choose a hash function to verify downloaded files today. Which one?

MD5 and SHA-1 have practical collision attacks and are deprecated. DES is an (obsolete) encryption algorithm, not a hash.

Q8True or false: SSL 3.0 and TLS 1.0 are still acceptable for protecting a modern website.

All SSL versions and TLS 1.0/1.1 are deprecated because of known attacks. Use TLS 1.3 (or 1.2 with strong cipher suites).

Q9On a 1–5 scale, risk A has likelihood 2 and impact 5; risk B has likelihood 4 and impact 3. Using risk = likelihood × impact, which do you treat first?

A = 2 × 5 = 10, B = 4 × 3 = 12. B scores higher, so it is the priority, although a very high-impact risk like A may still need a specific plan (for example insurance).

Q10An internet-facing server is missing a critical security patch. In risk terms, the missing patch is a…

It is a weakness. The threat is the attacker or malware that could exploit it; the risk is the likelihood and impact of that happening.

Q11How is a CCTV camera in a server room best classified?

A camera is physical and records or reveals intrusions (detective). A visible camera also deters, but it does not physically stop anyone entering.

Q12Which function was added in NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (2024)?

CSF 2.0 has six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Govern is new and covers strategy, roles, policy and supply-chain risk.

Q13The SolarWinds Orion compromise (2020) is the classic example of which kind of attack?

Attackers poisoned the vendor's build process, so customers installed the backdoor themselves through a trusted, signed update.

Q14A company buys cyber insurance to cover ransomware losses. Which risk treatment is that?

Insurance shifts the financial impact to a third party. It does not reduce the likelihood of an attack, so it is usually combined with mitigation.

Q15True or false: NotPetya victims who paid the ransom got their files back.

NotPetya was a wiper disguised as ransomware; recovery of the encrypted data was not possible even after payment. Offline backups were the only real remedy.

Q16How did attackers first get into Target's network in 2013?

A third-party HVAC vendor's credentials gave the first foothold. Poor segmentation and unheeded alerts let attackers reach payment systems: a multi-dimensional failure.

Summary

  • Cyberspace is a human-made extension of society; cybersecurity protects its assets with technical, organisational, human and legal measures.
  • DIC = CIA: availability, integrity, confidentiality. Identification, authentication, authorization, non-repudiation, accountability and traceability extend them.
  • Use phishing-resistant MFA (passkeys/FIDO2) where possible; SMS codes are the weakest form of MFA.
  • Logical and application security: secure SDLC, OWASP Top 10, patching, and current crypto (TLS 1.3, AES-256, SHA-256; not SSL, DES, MD5, SHA-1).
  • Operational security: inventory, configuration and patch management, incident response, tested and offline/immutable backups.
  • Threat ≠ vulnerability ≠ risk. Risk = likelihood × impact; treat by mitigating, transferring, avoiding or accepting.
  • Controls are preventive, detective or corrective, and administrative, technical or physical.
  • Frameworks: NIST CSF 2.0 (six functions incl. Govern), ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (93 Annex A controls), zero trust. Security is the strength of the weakest link.
